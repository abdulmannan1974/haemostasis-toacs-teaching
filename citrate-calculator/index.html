3 · Every formula, and where it comes from

ICSH 2021 · preferred

C = 1.85 × 10⁻³ × (100 − Hct) × V

C is the citrate (mL) to add to V mL of whole blood. Hct is entered as a percentage. Also given in CLSI H21-A5.

ICSH: This formula has been validated for use with PT, APTT and other coagulation tests, so should be preferred [1]. The validation is Marlar 2006, 28 patients with Hct 0.55–0.72 [2].

CLSI GP41 · Ingram and Hills

c = (100 − Hct) × S ÷ (595 − Hct)

c is the citrate left in the tube for a sample volume S. Quoted by ICSH from CLSI GP41-A6, and first described in Ingram and Hills' 1976 study of citrate concentration and the PT [1, 3].

ICSH: the two formulas give a citrate-to-blood ratio that is practically identical [1].

4 · Where 1.85 × 10⁻³ comes from

The constant is the normal 1 : 9 tube re-expressed per millilitre of plasma instead of per millilitre of blood, for a reference haematocrit of 0.40. This is algebra on the formula, shown so you can check it, not a quotation from a guideline.

  1. A standard tube holds 1 volume of citrate for every 9 volumes of blood, so citrate per mL of blood = 1 ÷ 9 = 0.111 mL.
  2. Citrate binds the calcium in the plasma, not in the red cells. At a haematocrit of 40%, 1 mL of blood holds 0.60 mL of plasma.
  3. So citrate per mL of plasma = 0.111 ÷ 0.60 = 0.185 mL.
  4. Any patient's plasma volume is V × (100 − Hct) ÷ 100. Keep 0.185 mL citrate per mL of that plasma: C = 0.185 × V × (100 − Hct) ÷ 100 = 1.85 × 10⁻³ × (100 − Hct) × V.
  5. Check: Hct 40%, V 4.5 mL gives 1.85 × 10⁻³ × 60 × 4.5 = 0.50 mL, the standard tube.

The CLSI GP41 formula does the same job anchored to a haematocrit of 0.45 and the total sample volume: Hct 45%, S 5.0 mL gives 55 × 5 ÷ 550 = 0.50 mL. Its 595 is 100 + 495, where 495 is plasma-per-citrate at that reference (0.5 mL citrate in 4.5 × 0.55 = 2.475 mL plasma, i.e. 1 : 4.95, ×100).

5 · Different tube sizes and neonatal samples

Tube size changes nothing in the formula

C scales in direct proportion to V, so use the draw volume printed on the tube. A 1.8 mL draw at Hct 0.65 needs 1.85 × 10⁻³ × 35 × 1.8 = 0.117 mL against the standard 0.20 mL, so remove about 0.08 mL. Small tubes forgive less: under-filling has a larger effect in small volume (pediatric) collection tubes [5], and ICSH still rejects any tube below 80% of nominal volume [1].

Practical steps

  1. Venous sample where possible. Dacie and Lewis: Venous blood samples should be obtained whenever possible, even from the neonate. [4]
  2. Know the haematocrit first, from a recent full blood count. ICSH asks for adjustment above 0.55 [1].
  3. Calculate C with the calculator, using the draw volume of the small tube.
  4. Remove the excess citrate before the draw. In the validating study a sterile tuberculin syringe was inserted through the stopper and the appropriate volume of anticoagulant removed, and the vacuum was kept so the tube still filled normally [2]. Magnette and colleagues give the same instruction [6].
  5. Label the prepared tube for that patient only. ICSH: a specially prepared tube should be labelled and handled in such a manner that will be only used for the intended patient [1].
  6. Fill to the mark (at least 90% is the usual target, never below 80%) [1, 5, 7], mix gently, and interpret against age-adjusted reference ranges [7].

Practical point, not from a guideline: removals in small tubes are often under 0.1 mL, so use a 1 mL syringe graduated in 0.01 mL and, where your laboratory prepares these tubes, record the volume removed on the tube.

6 · How strong is the evidence?

StudyDesignFinding
Marlar 2006 [2]28 patients, Hct 0.55–0.72, paired adjusted and unadjusted tubesPT and APTT differences exponentially increased with increasing hematocrit values. The basis of the ICSH recommendation.
Austin 2013 [8]40 samples with citrate added to simulate Hct 0.60no adjustment of citrate volume appears to be necessary in samples with hematocrits less than or equal to 60%
Silva 2020 [9]181 polycythaemic patients on warfarin, two PT reagentsRecombinant tissue factor INR needed no adjustment below Hct 0.62; with a rabbit-brain reagent, pairs differed by more than 10% above 0.55. The effect depends on the reagent.
Siegel 1998, via ICSH [1]Severe anaemiaICSH: there is usually no requirement to adjust the citrate-to-blood ratio for samples from subjects with anaemia.

Teaching line: ICSH says adjust above 0.55 because the error grows steeply with haematocrit. Between 0.55 and about 0.62 the size of the error depends on the reagent, which is why some laboratories validate a higher local threshold. Dacie and Lewis also adjusts for severe anaemia, but ICSH says this is usually unnecessary.
