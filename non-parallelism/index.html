Non-parallelism, explained in motion

A one-stage factor assay tests the patient's plasma at several dilutions and compares each clotting time with a reference plasma. If the patient really has a low factor, every dilution gives the same answer and the two lines run parallel. If something in the plasma is interfering with the test, the answer changes with dilution and the lines are not parallel. Pick a scenario and press Play.

Why dilution makes a lupus anticoagulant disappear

The one-stage assay needs phospholipid. A lupus anticoagulant is an antibody that interferes with that phospholipid step, so the clot forms slowly and the analyser reads too little factor VIII. Each dilution leaves less antibody in the tube, so its effect fades and the apparent factor level climbs towards the truth.

When the lines are not parallel

A lupus anticoagulant

The classic cause. Seen with lupus-sensitive APTT reagents when the lupus anticoagulant is high-titre; apparent factor activity rises as the antibody is diluted out (Practical Haemostasis).

"You cannot dilute nothing"

When the true factor level is below 1 IU/dL, clotting times are very prolonged and nearly identical at every dilution, so the line is flat. That is a severe deficiency, not interference (Practical Haemostasis).

Heparin or another anticoagulant

Listed by Friedman and colleagues among the causes, together with a specific factor inhibitor (Chapter 80).

A plotting or technical error

Check the data before calling interference: "the data has not been plotted correctly" is the first possibility Practical Haemostasis lists.

Does a specific factor VIII inhibitor cause it? The sources disagree.

Not in the original study. Ruinemans-Koerts and colleagues tested type 1 factor VIII inhibitors (19.5 and about 2 NBU/ml), type 2 inhibitors from acquired haemophilia (51.5 and about 2 NBU/ml) and a factor IX inhibitor (about 0.5 NBU/ml). All gave curves parallel to the reference, whatever the incubation time or temperature. Non-parallelism appeared only with high-titre lupus anticoagulants and lupus-sensitive reagents (PTT-LA, APTT-SP); Actin gave parallel lines.

But it can be seen. In Friedman and colleagues' case (Fig. 80.1, below), the patient with an acquired factor VIII inhibitor gives a non-parallel line, and the chapter lists specific inhibitors among the causes while citing Ruinemans-Koerts for the opposing view.

And it can appear in the other factor assays. Kurt and Fang describe a patient with a strong factor VIII inhibitor (126 BU) whose factor IX, XI and XII assays were non-parallel. A likely explanation, not stated in the abstract, is that the factor-deficient substrate plasma in those assays still contains factor VIII, which the patient's inhibitor can neutralise; diluting the patient's plasma dilutes the inhibitor.

For the exam: non-parallelism points to a high-titre lupus anticoagulant. A factor VIII autoantibody gave parallel lines in the primary study, so parallel lines do not exclude an inhibitor: do a mixing study and a Nijmegen-Bethesda assay.

What the laboratory does about it

  1. Test at least three dilutions and compare the patient line with the reference line. Friedman and colleagues note the World Federation of Hemophilia recommends parallel-line analysis, typically when the factor result is below 50%.
  2. If the lines are not parallel, the one-stage result must not be reported as a factor level: Practical Haemostasis says the results "should be regarded as incorrect".
  3. Repeat with a chromogenic assay, or with a lupus-insensitive (high-phospholipid) APTT reagent. If the repeat gives parallel lines, it is valid.
  4. Before calling interference, check the plotting, and remember a flat line at very long clotting times can be a true level below 1 IU/dL.
Exam-ready summary

In a one-stage factor assay, a genuine factor deficiency gives the same corrected level at every dilution, so the patient and reference lines are parallel. A lupus anticoagulant interferes with the phospholipid-dependent clot, so it lengthens the clotting time most in the least diluted tube; as the plasma is diluted the antibody is neutralised and the apparent factor level rises (for example 18, 46 and 82 IU/dL at 1/10, 1/20 and 1/40, with a normal chromogenic level). Non-parallel results are invalid: report the chromogenic or lupus-insensitive result instead. A flat line at very long clotting times can be a true level below 1 IU/dL.

Two corrections the check produced

Inhibitor kinetics are often labelled the wrong way round. Practical Haemostasis calls type I inhibitors "first-order" and type II "second-order". The original description (Gawryl and Hoyer 1982) gives type I as second-order with complete inactivation, and type II as complex with incomplete inactivation. Our Station 2 answer has been corrected to match.

A textbook worked example disagrees with itself. The Practical Haemostasis graph is parallel; the raw-data table printed beside it is not.
