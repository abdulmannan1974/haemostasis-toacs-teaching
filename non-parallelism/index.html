Non-parallelism, explained in motion

A one-stage factor assay tests the patient's plasma at several dilutions and compares each clotting time with a reference plasma. If the patient really has a low factor, every dilution gives the same answer and the two lines run parallel. If something in the plasma is interfering with the test, the answer changes with dilution and the lines are not parallel. Pick a scenario and press Play.

Why a lupus anticoagulant fades on dilution

The APTT reagent adds the same amount of phospholipid to every tube. A lupus anticoagulant binds to that phospholipid surface, through phospholipid-binding proteins, and gets in the way of the clotting complexes that assemble there. The clot forms slowly and the analyser reads too little factor VIII. Each doubling dilution halves the antibody, but the reagent phospholipid stays the same, so more of the surface is free. The interference fades and the apparent level climbs towards the true value.

When the lines are not parallel

A lupus anticoagulant

The classic cause. Seen with lupus-sensitive APTT reagents when the lupus anticoagulant is high-titre; apparent factor activity rises as the antibody is diluted out (Practical Haemostasis).

"You cannot dilute nothing"

When the true factor level is below 1 IU/dL, clotting times are very prolonged and nearly identical at every dilution, so the line is flat. That is a severe deficiency, not interference (Practical Haemostasis).

Heparin or another anticoagulant

Listed by Friedman and colleagues among the causes, together with a specific factor inhibitor (Chapter 80).

A plotting or technical error

Check the data before calling interference: "the data has not been plotted correctly" is the first possibility Practical Haemostasis lists.

Does a specific factor VIII inhibitor cause it? It depends which assay you look at.

In its own factor VIII assay: usually not, by the authors' reading. Ruinemans-Koerts and colleagues tested type 1 factor VIII inhibitors (19.5 and about 2 NBU/ml), type 2 inhibitors from acquired haemophilia (51.5 and about 2 NBU/ml) and a factor IX inhibitor (about 0.5 NBU/ml). The authors judged all of them parallel to the reference, whatever the incubation time or temperature, because these antibodies bind the factor essentially irreversibly; they add that parallelism does not exclude a factor antibody. Their own Figure 1B is less tidy. The two low-titre factor VIII inhibitors are parallel (slope ratio about 1.2). The 19.5 and 51.5 NBU/ml inhibitors run in the same direction but about 1.8 to 1.9 times as steep, and every one of their clotting times (102 to 173 s) is longer than the longest reference time (about 99 s), so none of those points can be read off the reference curve. Non-parallelism appeared only with high-titre lupus anticoagulants and lupus-sensitive reagents (PTT-LA, APTT-SP); Actin gave parallel lines.

In the other intrinsic factor assays: yes. The factor IX-, XI- and XII-deficient substrate plasmas contain normal factor VIII, which the patient's antibody neutralises. International recommendations: "A decrease in all intrinsic coagulation factors may be an in vitro false result arising from inhibitor-induced FVIII depletion in the substrate plasma" (Tiede 2020). The 2026 BSH/UKHCDO guideline extends the dilutions from 1/10–1/40 to 1/80–1/320, which "should correct the non-specifically reduced factors while the specifically reduced factor will remain low" (Percy 2026). Kurt and Fang describe exactly this: a strong factor VIII inhibitor (126 BU) with non-parallel factor IX, XI and XII assays.

And sometimes in its own assay. BSH 2020 notes that parallelism "may also be lost in patients with other immediate-acting inhibitors". Friedman and colleagues' teaching case (Fig. 80.1, below) shows a non-parallel factor VIII line in a patient with an acquired inhibitor, and the high-titre lines in Ruinemans-Koerts Figure 1B sit outside the reference curve altogether.

For the exam: a lupus anticoagulant makes every intrinsic factor rise with dilution. A factor VIII inhibitor keeps factor VIII low (usually parallel), but can make factors IX, XI and XII falsely low and non-parallel until they are diluted further, while the specific factor stays low. Parallel lines never exclude an inhibitor: do an incubated mixing study and a Nijmegen-Bethesda assay.

What the laboratory does about it

  1. Test at least three dilutions and compare the patient line with the reference line. Friedman and colleagues note the World Federation of Hemophilia recommends parallel-line analysis, typically when the factor result is below 50%. The Ruinemans-Koerts flow chart (Fig. 1D) moves to multiple dilutions whenever a single-dilution result falls below the lower limit of the reference range.
  2. If the lines are not parallel, the one-stage result must not be reported as a factor level: Practical Haemostasis says the results "should be regarded as incorrect".
  3. Repeat with a chromogenic assay (the next step in the Ruinemans-Koerts flow chart), or with a lupus-insensitive (high-phospholipid) APTT reagent. If the repeat gives parallel lines, it is valid. Then test for a lupus anticoagulant.
  4. If the lines are parallel but the level is low, mix the patient plasma 1:1 with buffered normal pooled plasma and incubate (1 h at 37 °C in the flow chart). No correction means an inhibitor: measure it with a Nijmegen-Bethesda assay. Correction does not close the case: the flow chart still flags a possible type 2 factor VIII inhibitor.
  5. Before calling interference, check the plotting, and remember a flat line at very long clotting times can be a true level below 1 IU/dL.
Exam-ready summary

In a one-stage factor assay, a genuine factor deficiency gives the same corrected level at every dilution, so the patient and reference lines are parallel. A lupus anticoagulant interferes with the phospholipid-dependent clot, so it lengthens the clotting time most in the least diluted tube; as the plasma is diluted the antibody is diluted out and the apparent factor level rises (for example 18, 46 and 82 IU/dL at 1/10, 1/20 and 1/40, with a normal chromogenic level). Non-parallel results are invalid: report the chromogenic or lupus-insensitive result instead, then test for a lupus anticoagulant. A factor VIII inhibitor behaves differently: factor VIII stays low, but factors IX, XI and XII can read falsely low and non-parallel until diluted further. Parallel lines do not exclude a factor inhibitor, because low-titre inhibitors gave parallel lines in the primary study. A flat line at very long clotting times can be a true level below 1 IU/dL.

Two corrections the check produced

Inhibitor kinetics are often labelled the wrong way round. Practical Haemostasis calls type I inhibitors "first-order" and type II "second-order". The original description (Gawryl and Hoyer 1982) gives type I as second-order with complete inactivation, and type II as complex with incomplete inactivation. Our Station 2 answer has been corrected to match.

A textbook worked example disagrees with itself. The Practical Haemostasis graph is parallel; the raw-data table printed beside it is not.
